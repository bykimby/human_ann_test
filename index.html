Annotation Guidelines

What you are making, and how to write it. How to drive the screen is in Tool guide.

1 · Why this data is being made

  • You are helping to build a dataset for research on multimodal document retrieval and question answering — systems that have to find the right page among many manuals and then read it to answer.
  • The results will be published as an academic paper.
  • The dataset itself is planned for public release, so the QA you write may be used by other researchers.
  • Your name and email are used only to keep track of your own work while the study runs. They are not included in the public dataset.

2 · The scenario this dataset assumes

Someone is using an electronic product and notices something on it — a button, an icon, a light, a part. They do not know what it is or what it does, so they describe what they see and ask.

A system then has to do the rest: find the right manual among many, find the page that shows that element, work out which element is meant, read the text beside it, and answer. Every QA you write is one test of exactly that.

  1. 1Brand & model → find the relevant manual
  2. 2Visual description → find the relevant page
  3. 3Identify the target visually
  4. 4Read the associated text → answer

Put briefly: a question written from what the product looks like, and an answer that needs both the picture and the text on the page.

3 · One finished example

A redrawn detail of a manual page — the real pages look like this. The boxed button is the image evidence; the shaded sentence, copied word for word, is the text evidence.

Question brand and model, then only what can be seen
On the Hyundai Sonata 2026, what does the small button with a curved windscreen outline and three wavy arrows rising towards it do?
Answer
It clears the fog from the inside of the windscreen.
Text evidence copied from the page, word for word
Press this button to defog the inside of the windscreen. The fan speed increases while it is on.
Image evidence what it looks like, without naming it
a small square button showing a curved windscreen outline with three wavy arrows rising towards it
Bounding box 0–1000, drawn by dragging on the page
[546, 298, 647, 338]
Around that one button — not the group it sits in, and not the whole figure. The numbers run 0–1000 across the whole page, so they are smaller than they look in this close-up.

Notice what the question does not say: it never calls the button a defroster. That word is the answer. If it appeared in the question, a system could find the page by searching for the word instead of by looking at the picture.

4 · What you have to write

One QA is finished when all five parts are filled in. Each has its own job:

  • Question — what the user asks. It carries the brand and model, and describes the target by sight alone. This is what sends the system looking.
  • Answer — one short sentence about the element the question describes. Finding it takes both: the picture tells you which element is meant, the page tells you what it is for.
  • Text evidence — the sentence on the page that supports the answer, copied word for word. It shows the answer came from the page and not from your own knowledge.
  • Image evidence — a few words describing what the target looks like, without naming it.
  • Bounding box — drawn around the image evidence only: the single element you just described. Not the text that answers the question, not the figure it belongs to, not the whole panel. It records which element the system had to find.

5 · The situation behind every question

You are looking at the manual. The person asking is not. They have the product in front of them, and finding the page is the system’s job.

  • Describe the target as it appears on the product: its shape, colour, icon, and where it sits on the device.
  • Never point to a place on the manual page — no figures, no pictures, no top-left or bottom-right of the page.
✗ A place on the manual page“the part in the figure at the top-left” · “in the bottom-right picture”
✓ A place on the product“the round button just left of the centre screen”

6 · How to annotate, step by step

  1. Choose a suitable page. You need a page that shows a clearly identifiable button, icon, component or part, and text on the same page explaining what it does. If a page has neither, move on: press Not suitable.
  2. Write the question. Name the brand and model, then describe the target by its look — shape, colour, icon, and where it sits on the product. Do not name it and do not say what it does.
  3. Write the answer. Read what the page says about that element and answer in one short sentence.
  4. Copy the text evidence. The sentence or phrase on the page that supports your answer, copied as printed. This is what proves the answer came from the page rather than from your own knowledge.
  5. Mark the image evidence. Describe the element in a few words without naming it, then drag a box around that element and nothing else. The box belongs to the image evidence alone — never draw it around the text you copied, and keep it tight rather than covering the whole figure.

Rules for the question

Every question must contain the brand and model, so the right manual can be found among all of them. Apart from that, describe the target using only what can be seen:

  • appearance or shape
  • colour
  • what the icon looks like
  • where the button or part sits on the product
  • how it relates to the things around it

Never use text taken from the page to point at the target: its name, its label, a menu name, the name of its function, or any other wording that identifies it directly.

✗ Names the target“What does the front windscreen defogger button do?”
the answer is already in the question
✓ Describes the target“What does the rectangular button with a curved screen icon and three wavy arrows do?”

Asking what it does is exactly right. What must not appear is the name of the thing or of its function — that is the answer.

7 · Choosing pages

  • Pages come from one shared pool, first come first served. Go through it and keep the pages that suit the task; pass over the rest with Not suitable. You do not have to use every page you open.
  • A page suits the task when it shows an element you can describe by sight alone, and says in text what that element does.
  • At most two QA per page, and the second must be about a different visual element, with its own answer, evidence and box.
  • Most pages come with a reference example — a finished QA for that page, with its answer, evidences and box. You may use it. If it already follows the rules, take it as your QA: copy its question and answer into the form, and press Show reference box on page to bring its box in. Check it first, and fix anything that breaks a rule — a question that names the target or points at the manual page is not acceptable just because the example did it. The example itself stays read-only; what counts is what you write.
  • If you write a second QA on the same page, it must be about a different element from your first one — including when your first was taken from the reference example.
  • Extra pages are pages added because the pages that come with a reference example may not be enough on their own. They have no reference example; instead they carry ambiguous spans, and you write the QA from the page itself, by the same rules.
  • An ambiguous span is a piece of text on the page that cannot be understood from the words alone — it only makes sense once you look at the picture beside it. “Press the button shown below” says nothing about which button; “set the switch to the position in the figure” says nothing about which position. The spans are listed in the form, and they point you at the elements on that page worth asking about.

8 · What to hand in

  • 50 or 100 completed QA — you choose which when you sign in, and the counter in the top left follows that goal.
  • If you want to change your goal afterwards, tell the coordinator first and agree it with them. Once they have agreed, press Switch and pick the other number; nothing you have written is affected.
  • A QA counts once it is marked complete, which needs all five parts: question, answer, text evidence, image evidence, and the box.
  • Before you mark it complete, read your question once more as if you had never seen the page: could you find this element from the words alone, without the page in front of you?

Using the annotation tool

What each part of the Annotate screen does. What to write, and why, is in Guidelines.

1 · Getting in

  • Open Annotate and sign in with your family name and given name in English, your email, and your goal — 50 or 100 QA. Use the same email every time: that is how your work comes back to you.
  • Load the image ZIP from the link your coordinator sent. The images stay in your browser and are never uploaded, so load the ZIP again on another computer or after clearing the browser.
  • Switch changes your name, email or goal — but agree any change of goal with the coordinator first. Sign out leaves the study on this browser; your saved work is untouched.

2 · The screen

  1. Your goal and progress: completed QA out of the 50 or 100 you chose.
  2. Filters for the list below. Extra appears only when the round has extra pages.
  3. The page list. One row per page: its number, the brand and manual, and a dot for its state.
  4. Box draws the bounding box; Pan drags the page around, as does holding Space. Shortcuts B and H.
  5. Zoom out, the current level, zoom in, and Fit to see the whole page.
  6. How many page images this browser has loaded from the ZIP.
  7. The manual page. Drag on it to draw the box around the element your question is about.
  8. The form for the QA you are writing.

3 · Choosing a page

  1. Your progress towards the goal you chose, and how many pages you have open.
  2. Filters: All, Open (free pages, and pages with room for another QA), Mine, Done, Skipped, Extra.
  3. The state dot — grey: open · blue: yours, in progress · green: completed by you · half amber and grey: someone finished QA 1 and there is room for a second · faded amber: taken · dashed ring: you pressed Not suitable.
  4. Pages marked extra have no reference example. Write the QA from the page itself; the ambiguous spans listed in the form are a hint.
  • Not suitable takes a page out of your list without blocking anyone else.
  • Next open page jumps to the next page you can work on. With the Extra filter on it stays among extra pages.

4 · Marking the element

  1. A dashed box is another QA on this page. Pick a different element; do not write about this one again.
  2. Your box. Drag a corner to resize, drag inside to move, and Clear in the form removes it. It goes around the image evidence only — that one element, not the text and not the whole figure.

5 · The form

  1. The QA on this page. Press a pill to read QA 1; + Add QA 2 appears when you may write the next one.
  2. The reference example for this page, if it has one. Open it to read, and take it as your own QA if it follows the rules — Show reference box on page copies its box into your form. The example itself cannot be edited.
  3. Question — as the person using the product would ask it, naming the brand and model.
  4. Answer — short, from the text on this page.
  5. Text evidence — the span of page text that supports the answer.
  6. Image evidence — what the target looks like, described without naming it.
  7. The checklist. Each line turns green when that part is done.
  8. Mark complete, or ⌘/Ctrl + Enter, once the checks are green.
  9. Not suitable if the page does not fit the task. It leaves your list and stays open to others.
  10. Next open page moves on without marking anything.

⚠ 6 · Sharing a page with someone else

  • A page can carry two QA, written by two different people. Once QA 1 is complete the page shows a half-filled dot, and anyone can open it and add QA 2.
  • You can read someone else's QA but you cannot edit it, and their box is drawn dashed on the page.
  • Do not create the same data again. Your QA must not repeat their question, and must be about a different visual element.
  • If you think their QA is wrong, do not write a corrected copy of it. Ask the coordinator to fix it, giving the page file name and the QA number.

7 · Saving, stopping, coming back

  • Everything is saved as you type; the header shows All changes saved. A QA becomes yours the moment you start typing it.
  • If the connection drops, keep working. What you type stays in this browser and goes up when you are back online.
  • Close the tab whenever you like. Sign in with the same email, on any computer, and your QA are there; only the image ZIP has to be loaded again.

8 · For the coordinator

  • Unlock Admin, then open Annotate without signing in, or press Sign out. That is admin review: every page and every QA is readable, including earlier rounds and pages hidden from annotators.
  • Any QA can be corrected or deleted there. A correction stays under its original author.
